Lesson 1

Introduction to Software Quality and SQA

Software quality is the degree to which a software product meets specified requirements (explicit or implicit) and satisfies the needs and expectations of its users and stakeholders.

Three views of quality

WhoDefinitionRemember
Philip B. Crosby (1926–2001), AmericanConformance to requirements: good quality if it does exactly what its specification says.Wrote “Quality Is Free”; promoted zero defects; prevention over inspection. Quote: “Quality is not an act, it is a habit.”
Joseph M. Juran (1904–2008), Romanian-AmericanFitness for use: must actually work well for the people who use it.Wrote Juran's Quality Control Handbook; Juran Trilogy = quality planning, quality control, quality improvement; vital few and trivial many; Pareto principle (80/20 rule). Quote: “Quality is not by chance; it must be planned.”
ISOFulfills inherent characteristicsISO = International Organization for Standardization
Example to know: A hospital records system can satisfy every line of its SRS (Crosby: conforms) yet be “low quality” if nurses find it confusing and slow during emergencies (Juran: poor fitness for use). Good quality needs both.

Quality is multi-dimensional (6)

  • Functionality – does what it is supposed to do
  • Reliability – works well without crashing or failing
  • Usability – people can learn and use it easily
  • Efficiency – uses memory, speed and computer power well
  • Maintainability – developers can fix bugs and update code easily
  • Security – data stays safe from threats and outside attacks

SQA and ISO standards

  • ISO 9001 – quality management systems, process control, continual improvement.
  • ISO/IEC 25010 – software product quality characteristics (reliability, usability, security, maintainability…).
  • ISO-based SQA emphasizes planning, reviews, documentation, traceability, and corrective action.

Cost of Quality (COQ)

A method of measuring the total cost of quality-related efforts and deficiencies, grouped into four categories:

  1. Prevention costs – prevent defects before they happen (training, quality planning, requirements review).
  2. Appraisal costs – detect defects through evaluation (testing, inspections, code reviews).
  3. Internal failure costs – defects found before delivery (rework, re-testing, debugging).
  4. External failure costs – defects found after delivery (warranty claims, customer support, reputation damage, legal liability). Almost always the most expensive.
Defect cost escalation: ₱100 to fix during requirements review → ₱1,500 during system testing → ₱15,000 after shipping to customers. The later a defect is found, the more it costs.
Sample classification: Nightly automated regression tests = Testing, Validation (code is executed), Appraisal cost (money spent to detect).
Lesson 2

Software Quality Models and Standards

Quality models give everyone a shared, measurable vocabulary. Two groups:

  • Product quality (judges the finished software): McCall's Model, ISO/IEC 25010:2023
  • Process & organizational quality (judges how it is built): CMMI, ISO 9001

McCall's Quality Model – 11 factors

Developed in 1977 by Jim McCall, Paul Richards, and Gene Walters for the U.S. Air Force (with General Electric). One of the first structured attempts to define quality in measurable terms. Three perspectives:

CategoryQuestionFactors
Product Operation (5)“Can I use it as it is?”Correctness (right results), Efficiency (good use of hardware/memory/time), Integrity (stops unauthorized access), Reliability (keeps performing correctly over time), Usability (easy to learn and operate)
Product Revision (3)“Can I fix, adapt, or improve it?”Maintainability (effort to locate and fix a bug), Flexibility (easy to change/extend), Testability (easy to write tests)
Product Transition (3)“Can I move it to a new environment?”Portability (runs on another OS/device), Reusability (parts reused in other projects), Interoperability (exchanges data with other systems)
Memory trick: Operation = C-E-I-R-U (“CEIRU”). Revision = M-F-T. Transition = P-R-I. Careful: Integrity is Operation, Interoperability is Transition.

ISO/IEC 25010:2023 Product Quality Model – 9 characteristics

Part of the SQuaRE series (Systems and software Quality Requirements and Evaluation). Published 15 November 2023 (2nd edition). The 2011 edition had 8 characteristics; 2023 has 9: two renamed, one new.

  1. Functional Suitability – completeness, correctness, appropriateness of functions
  2. Performance Efficiency – time behavior, resource use, capacity
  3. Compatibility – shares data and coexists with other software
  4. Interaction Capability – replaces “Usability” (includes users with disabilities)
  5. Reliability – availability, fault tolerance, recovery
  6. Security – confidentiality, integrity, accountability
  7. Maintainability – understand, fix, improve without breaking
  8. Flexibility – replaces “Portability” (adapt, scale, move platforms)
  9. Safety – NEW in 2023; avoids harm to people, property, environment

CMMI: Capability Maturity Model Integration

A process-improvement framework, currently maintained by ISACA (which acquired the CMMI Institute).

  • Staged representation – five predefined maturity levels, each building on the one before (focus of the lesson).
  • Continuous representation – rates individual process areas on their own capability scale; improve selectively.
LevelNameDescription
1InitialAd hoc, even chaotic; success depends on individual effort.
2ManagedBasic project management: planned, performed, measured, controlled; each project may do it its own way.
3DefinedProcesses documented as organization-wide standards, tailored per project.
4Quantitatively ManagedUses data and statistics to control process performance.
5OptimizingContinuous improvement becomes a habit; pursues innovation.

Memory trick: I Must Do Quality Often → Initial, Managed, Defined, Quantitatively Managed, Optimizing

ISO 9001 and software organizations

  • International standard for Quality Management Systems (QMS). Generic and industry-neutral; it does not prescribe software practices directly.
  • Software firms pair it with ISO/IEC 90003, the guideline that applies ISO 9001 to the software life cycle.
  • Requires planned, controlled “product realization”; regular internal audits; corrective and preventive action; competent staff and tools; continual improvement via PDCA (Plan–Do–Check–Act).
  • Benefits: fewer defects, stronger customer confidence, competitive differentiator when bidding for contracts.

Lesson 3

The SDLC and the Role of QA

The Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) is the structured sequence of stages a project moves through, from idea to retirement. Projects may loop back (e.g., Maintenance can surface new requirements).

The 7 standard phases

  1. Planning – purpose, scope, feasibility; project plan, cost/benefit and risk assessment.
  2. Requirements Analysis – gather user needs (interviews, surveys, workshops) → Software Requirements Specification (SRS).
  3. Design – architecture, data models, UI layouts → Software Design Document (SDD).
  4. Implementation – write code using coding standards, with peer code review.
  5. Testing – unit, integration, system, acceptance testing; defects sent back to developers.
  6. Deployment – release to production (strategies: big-bang, blue-green, canary), plus user training.
  7. Maintenance – patches, bug fixes, enhancements.

V-Model pairs (left side = Verification, right side = Validation, Coding at the bottom):

  • Requirements ↔ Acceptance Testing
  • System Design ↔ System Testing
  • Architecture Design ↔ Integration Testing
  • Module Design ↔ Unit Testing

Agile sprint cycle: Plan → Design → Develop → Test → Review.

Shift-Left testing

Shift-left means moving QA activities earlier in the SDLC instead of concentrating testing at the end. Benefits: cost efficiency, better quality (less technical debt), speed without sacrifice.

  • Planning – QA defines measurable quality goals and testable objectives
  • Requirements – reviewed for clarity, completeness, testability
  • Design – design/architecture reviews; test strategy planned
  • Implementation – unit tests, static code analysis, often TDD (test-driven development)
  • Testing – integration, system, regression, UAT
  • Deployment – release verification and smoke tests; monitoring
  • Maintenance – every patch gets regression-tested

Lesson 4

Fundamentals of Software Testing

Software testing is a set of activities (planning, analysis, design, implementation, execution, evaluation) used to determine whether a work product satisfies specified requirements, to demonstrate it is fit for purpose, and to detect defects. Testing is a process, not a single act.

Test process (5): Test planning & control → Test analysis & design → Test implementation → Test execution → Test completion/reporting. Execution is only one of the five.

Error → Defect → Failure

  • Error (mistake) – a human action that produces an incorrect result (developer misremembers the overtime rule).
  • Defect (fault / bug) – the flaw left in a work product (calculateOvertime() multiplies every hour by 1.5×).
  • Failure – the defect is triggered and produces a visible wrong result (wrong paycheck).

Not every defect causes a failure; it shows only when the flawed step is executed.

8 objectives of testing

  1. Evaluate work products
  2. Trigger failures & find defects
  3. Ensure required coverage
  4. Reduce risk of poor quality
  5. Verify requirements are met
  6. Verify compliance (laws, standards, contracts)
  7. Inform stakeholder decisions
  8. Build confidence

Testing vs Debugging

TestingDebugging
Finds and reports that a defect exists; does not fixFinds the root cause of a known defect and changes the code to fix it
Done by testers, developers, business analystsDevelopment activity, done by the developer
Planned part of the formal test processFollows testing; triggered by a reported failure

Analogy: testing = doctor's diagnosis; debugging = surgeon's repair.

The 7 testing principles

  1. Testing shows the presence of defects – cannot prove software is defect-free (clean blood test analogy).
  2. Exhaustive testing is impossible – use risk analysis and techniques (10 fields × 5 values = 9.7 million combos).
  3. Early testing saves time and money – fix in requirements, not production (essay typo analogy).
  4. Defects cluster together – few modules hold most defects; Pareto 80/20 (dangerous intersections analogy).
  5. Beware the pesticide paradox – same tests repeated stop finding new defects; review and update tests.
  6. Testing is context dependent – hospital system vs puzzle game (paper airplane vs jet).
  7. Absence-of-errors is a fallacy – bug-free but wrong/unusable product still fails (too-short ladder).

Static testing (without executing code)

Examines work products (requirements, designs, code) without running them. Two families: reviews (people) and static analysis (tools: unreachable code, syntax violations, security vulnerabilities). Analogy: proofreading.

Four levels of review formality (least → most formal):

  1. Informal review – no defined process, often no written record.
  2. Walkthrough – the author leads peers through the work product.
  3. Technical review – trained peers/experts, checklist, technical content only, no managers.
  4. Inspection – most formal: defined roles (author, moderator, reviewers), checklists, metrics, formal entry/exit criteria.

Dynamic testing (executing code)

Running the software with test cases and comparing actual vs expected results. Analogy: test-driving a car. Levels: unit, integration, system, acceptance.

Independence in testing

Authors carry assumptions and blind spots; independent testers bring fresh eyes. Benefits: different defects found, assumptions verified, objective assessment. Drawbacks: isolation, developers lose ownership (“that's the tester's job”), testers may lack domain knowledge.

  1. No independence – developers test their own code
  2. Within the team – testers embedded in the dev team
  3. Independent test team – separate QA department, same company
  4. Business / user community – real users (e.g., UAT)
  5. External / outsourced – outside firm (e.g., penetration testing)

Most organizations operate at Levels 2–3 and reserve 4–5 for critical releases.

System testing

Purpose: verify requirements, check quality attributes (ISO/IEC 25010), find end-to-end defects, reduce risk and build confidence.

Functional = WHAT the system does. Non-functional = HOW WELL: performance, security, usability, reliability, compatibility.

7 steps: Plan → Design → Prepare → Execute → Report → Retest & Regress → Close. Repeat until exit criteria are met.

  • Entry criterion example: integration testing completed, build installed.
  • Exit criterion example: all high-priority tests executed, no open critical defects.

Acceptance testing forms (ISTQB, 5)

  1. User Acceptance Testing (UAT) – intended users confirm it fits business processes; ends in formal sign-off; typical for custom systems.
  2. Operational acceptance testing – IT operations staff check backup, recovery, installation, maintenance, security procedures.
  3. Contractual & regulatory – against contract criteria or laws (e.g., Data Privacy Act).
  4. Alpha testing – at the developer's site by potential users/independent team (not the developers), near end of development. Analogy: taste-testing in the kitchen.
  5. Beta testing – real users in their own environment, after alpha, before final release. Analogy: soft opening.
Lesson 6

Black-Box Test Design Techniques

Black-box testing designs tests from the specification only, without looking at internal code. Also called specification-based testing (ISTQB) or behavioral testing. Usable at all test levels. Based on requirements, user stories, use cases, business rules. Analogy: a vending machine.

1. Equivalence Partitioning (EP)

Divide inputs into partitions where all values are processed the same way; test one representative value per partition. There are valid and invalid partitions. Analogy: sorting laundry.

4 steps: Identify the input → Find the partitions → Pick one value each → Write test cases. Test each invalid partition in its own test case so one error does not hide another.

Example, age 18–60: below 18 (10, invalid), 18–60 (35, valid), above 60 (75, invalid) → 3 tests.

2. Boundary Value Analysis (BVA)

Tests the edges (min and max) of partitions, where bugs like < instead of <= hide. Only for ordered partitions (numbers, dates, lengths). Used together with EP. Analogy: speed limit sign.

VersionValues testedAge 18–60
2-value BVABoundary + closest outside neighbor17, 18, 60, 61 (4 values)
3-value BVABoundary + both neighbors17, 18, 19, 59, 60, 61 (6 values)

Example, quantity 1–10: EP value 5; BVA 0 (reject), 1, 10 (accept), 11 (reject).

3. Decision Table Testing

For behavior depending on combinations of conditions (business rules). Lists conditions and actions; each column is a rule = one test case. Number of rules = 2ⁿ for n yes/no conditions; reduce with “don't care” (–).

4. State Transition Testing

For systems whose behavior depends on the current state and past events (the same input gives different results). Modeled as a state transition diagram or table. Tests both valid and invalid transitions. Analogy: traffic light. Example: ATM PIN entry (3 wrong PINs → card retained).

  • State – condition the system is in (Waiting for PIN)
  • Event – something that happens (Enter PIN)
  • Transition – change from one state to another
  • Action – what the system does during a transition (show “Try again”)
  • Guard – optional condition that must be true ([PIN is correct])

ISTQB notation: event [guard] / action. Coverage: all states, all valid transitions (0-switch), all transitions including invalid.

Test Case Documentation

Test Plan

A test plan describes the scope, approach, resources, and schedule of testing: what, how, by whom, when, and the risks. Analogy: planning a field trip.

Standards timeline: IEEE 829 first published 1983 → IEEE 829-1998 (classic 16-section test plan) → 829-2008 revision → 2013 ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-3 replaces IEEE 829 → 2021 current edition.

Test Case

A test case is a set of preconditions, inputs (test data), actions (steps), expected results, and postconditions, developed to check one specific condition or requirement. Analogy: a recipe card (the photo of the dish = expected result, decided before cooking).

12 components: Test Case ID, Title/Description, Requirement ID, Module/Feature, Priority, Preconditions, Test Data, Test Steps, Expected Result, Actual Result, Status, Prepared/Executed by & Date. (Actual Result and Status are filled in during execution.)

Status values: Pass / Fail / Blocked / Not Run.

Good test case qualities: clear & specific, one purpose, traceable, repeatable, independent, measurable result. A poor test case has no ID or requirement link, vague steps, no test data, and an untestable expected result (“System works properly”).

Requirements Traceability Matrix (RTM)

A table linking each requirement to the test cases that verify it, usually with execution status and defects. A requirement with no test case is a gap. Supports impact analysis and shows progress. Analogy: packing checklist.

  • Forward traceability – Requirement → Test cases. “Is every requirement tested?”
  • Backward traceability – Test case → Requirement. “Why does this test exist?”
  • Bidirectional – both directions; most complete and most commonly used.

White-box coverage (from the key takeaways)

White-box testing designs tests from the code's structure. Coverage strength: Statement < Branch/Decision < Path. 100% branch coverage guarantees 100% statement coverage, but not the reverse. Full path coverage is often impractical.
